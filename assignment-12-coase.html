Coase and The Problem of Social Cost: My Notes

My answers to 21 questions on Ronald Coase, "The Problem of Social Cost," Journal of Law and Economics 3 (1960), pp. 1–44, plus the moments my thinking changed along the way.

Answers by question

1. Why "make the polluter pay" is the wrong framing; what a social cost is

My answer: We should care for most total value produced for a situation. If we shut down the factory then the factory is harmed possibly more than the neighbors with all the production it would lose. In this case, we care more about stopping serious harm than smaller ones. Social cost is the impact that an action has on everything around it. The factory has a social cost of polluting the surrounding neighborhoods, but stopping the smoke has a cost of the factory’s lost production.
Example I used: Factory smoke harming the neighbors.
Where in the paper: Sections I and II, “The Reciprocal Nature of the Problem” (pp. 1-3)

2. Minimum evidence that pollution is inefficient; is pollution itself "bad"?

My answer: The marginal benefit that the neighbors get from the smoke cut should be greater than the cost or loss of production that the factory makes. The thing is that the neighborhoods can take a little bit of smoke, they do not need to have zero pollution, just enough so that they do not have grey skies or get cancer. It’s hard to change anything though because gauging the cost of a monetary thing like production and a human want like health will lead to estimates and complicated ranges of outcomes. So to find inefficiency you have to find a trade that would make total value better but is not happening. That’s the minimum evidence required.

3. Would damaged parties change behavior because they'd be compensated?

My answer: Yes, if there is an incentive for the farmer to plant more crops because that would mean more of a payout when the cattle trample over it, the farmer would do it. So if you just pay him to not plant, he puts almost zero cost in and retains a benefit with cash, which he could potentially do something else with. It doesn’t matter though because the outcome will be the same. The farmer’s threats will only change if he gets paid or if the rancher keeps all of the gains. The distribution changes, not the production. (Section III)

4. Removing the liability rule when the price system works smoothly

My answer: Now the farmer has to pay so that the rancher stops trampling over his crops, either by not having more cattle or putting up a fence. The rancher’s herd actually stays the same because the farmer pays so that there are not any more steer. This turns into an opportunity cost for the rancher, which leads to the same efficient outcome no matter which side the law gives the right to, as long as bargaining costs nothing. The law still has to define who holds the right first, or there is nothing to bargain over. As stated by Coase, “But the ultimate result (which maximises the value of production) is independent of the legal position if the pricing system is assumed to work without cost” (Section IV).

5. Did Coase say "liability institutions don't matter"?

My answer: No, in fact he says the opposite. For one, we still need rights to know what to trade. The law is like the beginning rules on value, and bargaining hashes out the details to efficiency. There is also the reality that deals will cost more than just the deal itself. Transaction costs decide whether a deal happens, and when it doesn't, whoever the law gave the right to decides the outcome. The takeaway really is not that laws don’t matter, but to compare when it makes sense to make an arrangement. (End of Section IV, Section VI)

6. Why polluter-pays isn't automatically correct: one case study

My answer: Case: Sturges v. Bridgman (Section V). These are two good uses of an area, but we have to decide which is better for value. The doctor is doing harm to the candy store by telling them to stop using the machine, while the confectioner is harming the doctor with the vibrations. At this point, each could pay for the other to stop doing the action that is causing the “pollution,” but it may be worth more to have their systems the way they are and leave it to another authority. The courts are not only looking for value but also the nuance of the case. In this instance, the fancy neighborhood would care more about having less noise than the candy machine would make than a standard neighborhood would. Also, with the other doctors’ offices, it was a better fit for doctors to use that space. The court does not decide who gets the land; whoever values it more will decide that. The court only decides who pays whom.

7. What #6 has to do with sustainability

My answer: Sustainability is having both parties be satisfied with the result. That doesn’t mean we fully stop the cost or harm done to one or another, but we offset it to a point where both can last. The result is only unsustainable if shutting down the candy store destroyed more value than it saved, for example if the candy-making was worth more but bargaining was too costly for the confectioner to buy the right back. Whether he gets compensated is about distribution, not sustainability.

8. Sturges: does the doctor destroy the confectioner's way of life, or promote progress?

My answer: It’s a change in what is most valuable for that space right now. If the confectioner really cared about that space, he could offset the value added from the doctor’s office or pay equal to the amount of noise pollution he creates. In the end it doesn’t really matter who bears the cost or benefits, as long as a society we are at efficiency. You can put your eggs in one basket or many, but all that matters is the amount of eggs.

9. Sturges, the metal forges, the local-farms flyer and zoning: what do they have in common?

My answer: The character does absolutely cost. Even at opportunity cost, you choose quiet doctors’ offices over a long-time candy shop. Rizzo’s flyers are choosing local farms and jobs over cheaper products with more variety that are imported. Even the townspeople are choosing more rigid neighborhoods over affordable housing. Instead of paying the monetary cost of those actions that the other party feels, they use government and majority pressure to change who bears the cost to favor them. The town is supposed to be the non-partisan decision maker for all, but they also have internal biases.

10. Densely populated cities: defend Part A (bigger government) and Part B (smaller government)

Part A: With more people come more conflicts in much smaller spaces. There is more life, but more control is needed to sort out all that life and humanity. That is why we need more government to handle the growth of problems. It’s not necessarily worse, just grander. There are more transaction costs that make deals not happen, which is why we need government.
Part B: With denser cities the social costs get spread out, so bargains are far easier to create. You are fighting for a group of people instead of an individual party, people that just want less air pollution or more jobs, not specific scenarios that require individual government intervention. Even so, our big government exists to put a drag on all of it. One party bargains for many, so that stops the transaction costs that made government required in Part A.
The 4 implicit claims: (1) Likelihood of conflict: A thinks more people means more conflict; B thinks more people means more people fighting the same issues but not greater cost. Greater distribution but same production. (2) Ease of resolution: in A, bargaining gets harder with more complicated transaction costs; B says with organized parties, conflicts get solved more easily. (3) Size of the pie: more wealth does lead us to buy more government to control where that wealth goes, but it also should not matter, because all government does is handle who pays what and the basics, and most people sort out the rest. (4) Role of government: in A it grows to handle the increased social costs and as an alternative for larger transaction costs; in B it shrinks because issues become general and are handled by organizations, with many aspects of paying and creating sustainability.

11. Does Coase ever say transaction costs ARE zero?

My answer: No, it’s just a thought experiment. In the real world there are transaction costs of making deals happen. The idea is that when pricing fails, or the transaction costs of doing a deal are too much, we may need government or another arrangement to be able to look at the full picture to find the optimal placement of resources for the most value. The zero-cost world is great for showing how a world would be without transaction costs, where prices could mostly dictate the social optimum by themselves. (Sections IV and VI)

12. Theory of the firm, regulation, and the "super-firm"

My answer: Firms form when it is cheaper to bring people into an organization than to deal with them on their own in the market. Government is a super version of this, with its power superseding the rules of the market. That is helpful when bargaining fails over pollution costs. The difference is that a normal firm has to perform well or someone will beat it through competition. The only thing to check a government is itself, and the timeline is usually much longer and it is much harder to determine who’s at fault. (Section VI)

13. Courts and "common sense": are courts' decisions unimportant?

My answer: Courts’ decisions are still very important in the real world because of transaction costs. When deals are unable to be made, the court's ruling decides who gets to use the resource. But a major distinction is that courts look to what helps the future the most: what precedent you are setting with these actions about rights and trades. If common sense is wrong, then it leaves an unstable basic layer to do trade and negotiation on, and prices fail. That is why you cannot just look at the gained or lost value alone. (Section VII)

14. Page 26: the source of many nuisances; local examples; the irony

My answer: The government is often the one that creates harmful activities, “pollution” like highways or utilities, and makes them unable to be sued for damages caused by these projects. Pigou’s fix is to have more government, but Coase is saying that government is the cause of many of these issues. (p. 26)
Local example: We just had an issue of abandoned cats bringing fleas into the backyards of my neighborhood. These cats bring fleas and disease that have been scary for our pets. Nobody owns the cats, so there is no one to bargain with or sue. With no clear property rights, the market can't fix it, which is why people end up calling the town.

15. The U.S. military, green energy projects, ethanol

My answer: As stated before, government projects are often exempt from scrutiny from the courts, so they do not have the chance to weigh the benefit over cost. So the cost goes directly to the people affected by the externalities from the projects. The only people that go against these projects are political opponents, and that gets swept aside as just he-says-she-says.

16. "The People's Romance": what Coase accuses Pigou of

My answer: Coase has a problem with the potential fallacy that the government can fix everything. Pigou makes the assumption that the government’s actions are not costly and never fail, such as with the railway sparks. He treated the situation as a market problem because the railway did not pay. Coase shows that the government made the law that allowed the railway not to pay. (Section VIII)

17. The irony of Pigou missing reciprocity

My answer: Pigou’s paper is about finding the costs that we miss. Yet he only studied private and social costs and only counted the harm to the farmers, never the cost to the side being stopped. He never thought of the cost of not having the railway.

18. Boulston's case, carbon taxes, and "you didn't build that"

My answer: Following Boulston’s case, once the carbon leaves the area then it’s not the producer’s anymore, like the rabbits. It becomes public domain for everyone to feel the effects of. The point is that we need to decide fully whether the producer owns or doesn’t own what they make, good or bad. Based on that decision, we need to fully comprehend the rights to that stuff. (Section VIII)

19. How Coase shows Pigou's analysis AND his policy conclusions were wrong

My answer: Coase showed that Pigou only looked at one side’s cost and never the polluter’s. His policies were all to the detriment of the polluter and never stopped to figure out what the other party’s cost would be. Just because the tax will help stop pollution from the factory does not mean it’s the right thing to do. You have to look at the total value each party gets. (Sections IX and X)

20. The methodological failure Coase identifies

My answer: Economists look at the perfect, ideal scenario and try to compare it with the real, inefficient market. Coase suggests comparing the real options to find the cost. That includes firms and the government, to find the total net benefit. This still happens today when the government does everything in its power to keep home prices high even though a lot of homeowners are older, while the rest of the people who want a home have no ability to have one. (Section X)

21. Factors of production: the usual view vs. Coase's view, and why it matters

My answer: The usual view is that a factor of production is something physical, like land or machines, while Coase views these factors as someone’s right to do something. The right usually entails both the cost of doing something and the benefits. It matters because once you see pollution as just a factor, you realize there are other ways to deal with it than just getting rid of it, especially if the action causes more benefit than the overall cost done. (Section X)
