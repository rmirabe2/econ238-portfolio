# Come Get Your Gas, or I'll Light It on Fire 🔥
### Leak it, lose it: foreclosing on methane leaks to power AI and save ~80,000 lives a year.

## The idea in one paragraph

The oil, gas and coal industries leak about **124 million tonnes of methane** a year. Methane traps about 80 times more heat than CO₂ over 20 years, and the leaked gas is worth billions. Companies let it escape because **once gas leaks into the air, nobody owns it**, so leaking costs them nothing. We propose a new property rule: **leaked methane is abandoned property.** If you let it escape, you have a short window to fix the leak. If you don't, you lose the gas, and someone else gets to capture it, usually a data center that turns it into computing power. Leaks no one bids on get **set on fire** (cleanly, away from homes), and the tiniest leaks are left alone.

**Feed. Burn. Leave.**

---

## 1. The hook: the Gates of Hell

In 1971, Soviet scientists set fire to a gas crater in Turkmenistan's Karakum Desert, expecting it to burn out in weeks. It has burned for more than 50 years and became the country's most famous tourist site. Now Turkmenistan is choking off its gas, and the crater's heat has dropped more than 75% in three years (Capterio).

The irony: **the flame was never the problem.** Turkmenistan tops satellite lists of the world's worst methane leakers (IEA, 2026). The damage comes from the leaks nobody can see, because nobody lit them.

> A burning hole in the desert does far less damage than an invisible leak. So build more Gates of Hell, just not next to anyone's house.

## 2. The problem: a property-rights failure

| | Methane per year (2025) |
|---|---|
| Oil operations | 45 Mt |
| Coal mining | 43 Mt |
| Natural gas | 36 Mt |
| **Total** | **124 Mt** (IEA Global Methane Tracker 2026) |

- **The fixes exist and often pay.** About 70% of fossil methane, roughly 85 Mt, can be cut with existing technology. **Nearly 30 Mt of upstream oil and gas emissions could be cut at no net cost** at 2025 gas prices (IEA).
- **Companies still don't bother.** Escaped gas belongs to no one, so leaking costs them nothing. Remote wells often have no pipeline, so the gas is worth $0 to the operator.
- **Fines haven't worked.** In Nigeria's Niger Delta, companies have cut flaring only about 10% since 2007, preferring to pay fines rather than stop.

The problem isn't technology. **It's that no one owns the leak.**

---

## 3. Why it seems crazy, and why it might not be (back-of-the-envelope)

### Why it seems crazy
- "You want to **set things on fire** to help the climate?"
- "You want to **confiscate gas** from private companies?"
- "You want to **stop plugging most old wells**?"
- "You want **AI**, the energy villain, to be the cleanup crew?"

### Why it might not be

**a) Fire beats leaking, by a lot.** Burning 1 tonne of methane makes 2.75 tonnes of CO₂. Leaked unburned, it warms like 82.5 tonnes of CO₂ over 20 years (IPCC AR6).

| Per tonne of methane | 20-year warming (t CO₂e) | vs. leaking |
|---|---|---|
| Let it leak | 82.5 | — |
| Flameless catalytic oxidizer (~85% destroyed) | 14.7 | 5.6× better |
| Real-world flare (91% destroyed; Plant et al. 2022) | 9.8 | 8.4× better |
| Perfect burn | 2.75 | 30× better |

**b) The leaked gas could power the AI boom.**
- The IEA estimates ~100 billion m³ of leaked gas is recoverable every year.
- That's ~68 Mt of methane = 944 TWh of heat → **~380 TWh of electricity** at 40% generator efficiency.
- All data centers on Earth used **415 TWh** in 2024 (IEA). **The gas the world leaks could run about 90% of today's data centers.**

**c) Fixing every leak is a bad deal; fixing the big ones is a great deal.**
- An average unplugged U.S. orphaned well leaks ~0.13 t of methane a year (EPA). That's ~15 grams an hour: about **one 80-watt light bulb** of power and **$18 of gas a year**.
- Plugging it costs a median **$20,000**, or **$76,000** with land cleanup (Raimi et al. 2021). For measured wells, plugging cost more than the climate benefit in **75–80%** of cases (Raimi & Cilento, RFF 2026).
- Meanwhile, the leakiest 9–16% of unplugged wells cause **over 90%** of their methane (Williams, Regehr & Kang 2021).
- **One satellite-detected super-emitter (1 t/hour) leaks as much as ~67,000 average wells.** In one Permian Basin study, super-emitters caused roughly half of all emissions (IEA 2026).

**Lesson:** don't chase every hole. Go after the giants, and make the owners of the giants care.

## 4. The plan: "Leak it, lose it"

### The rule
**Any methane that escapes is abandoned property.** This puts the same pressure on active and inactive sites.

### Step by step
1. **Detect.** Satellites (MethaneSAT, Carbon Mapper, the UN's MARS system) and inspections flag leaks and put them on a public list.
2. **Redeem or foreclose.** The owner gets ~60 days to fix the leak and keep its property.
   - **Active sites** (oil fields, pipelines, coal mines): if the owner doesn't act, foreclosure works like a **lien**. A licensed third party, chosen by auction, installs capture or burning equipment **at the owner's expense**, and the owner loses the gas. Fines still apply.
   - **Abandoned sites** (orphaned wells, closed mines): there's no owner to redeem, so they go **straight to auction**, with no inherited liability.
3. **Feed.** Winning bidders, often modular data centers, capture the gas and use it. *Moving data is cheaper than moving gas.*
4. **Burn.** Sites with no bidder are destroyed if the leak is big enough. Mostly this means coal mine ventilation air, burned in enclosed oxidizers.
5. **Leave.** Leaks too small to burn are left alone, unless they're near homes or water.

**"The best foreclosure is the one that never happens."** For most oil and gas leaks, fixing is cheap or profitable. Once losing the gas is a real threat, most owners will simply fix it themselves.

### The fire ranking: where the flame is allowed
| Rank | What happens to the gas | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Use it** | Sold, or burned inside a generator to power computing | Anywhere; **required near homes** |
| 2. **Destroy it cleanly** | Enclosed combustors or flameless oxidizers | Coal mine vents, medium leaks |
| 3. **Open flare** | Last resort | Only far from people |
| 4. **Vent it** | **Never** | — |

This plan **reduces** open flaring: routine flaring of sellable gas moves up to rank 1.

### What happens in each sector
| Sector | Share | Most likely outcome | Who pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oil and gas, active | 65% | Owners fix leaks themselves to avoid losing the gas | The owner, often at a profit |
| Oil and gas, remote (no pipeline) | — | Data centers take the stranded gas | The bidder, which profits |
| Coal mine ventilation air | ~35% for all coal | Too thin to sell (<1% methane), so it's burned in giant oxidizers (>95% destroyed) | Mine owner (lien) or auction revenue |
| Coal mine drained gas | — | Concentrated, so it's sold or powers data centers | The bidder |
| Abandoned coal mines | ~3% | Auction; if no bid, burn | Bidder or auction revenue |
| Orphaned wells | ~0.3% | Burn the medium ones, leave the tiny ones | Auction revenue |

### Rules for auction winners
1. **No new holes.** No drilling or pumping to boost flow; output is capped at the measured leak rate.
2. **Gas stays on site** to power the data center; it can't become a new gas field.
3. **Pay for results.** The claim lasts only while satellites and meters show the leak destroyed; if it returns, the winner loses the claim and its bond.
4. **Bond and plug-out fund.** Part of the revenue is saved to permanently seal the site when the gas runs out.
5. **Fixed terms.** The lease ends when flow drops too low, and the site drops to Burn.

### The money loop
Profitable sites (oil and gas, drained coal gas) generate auction revenue. That revenue pays to burn the unprofitable ones (coal ventilation air, orphaned wells) and goes partly to the communities living next to the leaks. Fully destroying coal mine methane worldwide would cost **under $4 billion** (Ember, 2026).

## 5. How much does this stop?

Our estimate, using IEA figures and stated assumptions:

| | Low | Middle | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oil and gas (81 Mt) | 30% reached, 90% destroyed → 22 Mt | 50% (≈ super-emitter share) → 37 Mt | 75% (IEA technical max) → 61 Mt |
| Coal (43 Mt) | 35% → 15 Mt | 45% → 19 Mt | 50% (IEA technical max) → 22 Mt |
| Orphaned wells (0.4 Mt) | ~0.3 Mt | ~0.3 Mt | ~0.3 Mt |
| **Methane stopped** | **37 Mt (30%)** | **56 Mt (45%)** | **83 Mt (67%)** |
| 20-year warming avoided, per year | 3.1 Gt CO₂e | 4.6 Gt CO₂e | 6.8 Gt CO₂e |
| Gas recovered ($3–10/MMBtu) | $5–18B/yr | $8–27B/yr | $12–39B/yr |
| Premature deaths avoided (ozone) | ~53,000/yr | **~80,000/yr** | ~118,000/yr |

For comparison, the Global Methane Pledge aims for a 30% cut by 2030. Deaths: the UN's Global Methane Assessment estimates each million tonnes of methane cut prevents ~1,430 premature deaths a year from ozone.

**Why not 100%?** Satellites only see big leaks, some coal methane can't be captured with today's technology, and burning isn't perfect.

## 6. Proof it can work

- **Nigeria already did a version of this.** Its Gas Flare Commercialisation Programme takes gas that oil companies were flaring and auctions it to third parties. **49 flare sites were auctioned to 42 bidders**, expected to capture 250–300 million cubic feet of gas a day and cut ~6 million tonnes of CO₂ a year. Permits were issued in December 2025, after the program sat dormant for seven years.
- **Crusoe** parked mobile data centers on oil wells that were burning off gas, cutting methane ~98% versus flaring. In 2025 it sold that business, 425 modular data centers, to NYDIG to chase giant AI campuses. Lesson: it pays, but the computer must match the leak.
- **Germany** captures and uses about **99%** of the methane from its abandoned coal mines, mostly for electricity (Global Energy Monitor).
- **Coal mine oxidizers** have destroyed **over 95%** of ventilation-air methane in U.S. demonstrations.
- **North Dakota** oil companies captured **95.5%** of Bakken gas in a recent month once pipelines and rules caught up.

## 7. Who pays for leaks today

- **The Niger Delta, Nigeria:** about 2 million people live within 4 km of a gas flare, and World Bank researchers link flaring to illness among young children. In **Oyigbo**, flaring rose about 15× after Shell sold the field, while Shell reported zero routine flaring on paper. Responsibility followed the owner, not the leak.
- **The Fort Berthold Reservation, North Dakota:** home of the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara Nation, it saw about 20% of all North Dakota flaring from 2012 to 2020. Tribal members own royalties on that gas and lose millions when it's burned, while breathing the smoke. Across three U.S. flaring basins, ~535,000 people live within 3 miles of flares.

**"Leak it, lose it" ties the right to the gas to the site, so a sale can't make the problem disappear on paper, and auction revenue goes partly to these communities.**

## 8. Economic analysis

### Incentives
| Player | Today | Under "Leak it, lose it" |
|---|---|---|
| Leak owner | Leaking is free | Leak → lose the gas, pay for the fix, plus fines |
| Absent or bankrupt owner | No one responsible | Auction winner profits from capping |
| Data centers | Wait years for grid power | Cheap power available now |
| Governments | Pay $20k–76k per old well | Collect auction revenue; spend it on the leaks that matter |
| Front-line communities | Live with the smoke | Get a share of auction revenue; open flares banned near homes |

### Why hasn't anyone done this? (Chesterton's fence)
1. **Escaped gas has no owner**, so no one has standing to claim it.
2. **Liability: touch it, you own it.** Cleaning up a polluted site can mean inheriting its liability. The U.S. Good Samaritan Act (2024) shields volunteers, but allows only 15 cleanups in a 7-year pilot and bans profiting.
3. **You couldn't see the leaks.** Satellites that spot individual leaks from space are new.
4. **No pipelines** at many remote wells, so the gas seemed worthless.
5. **The fence has real purposes:** plugging protects groundwater (so we still plug near homes and water), and flares exist for safety (so emergency flaring stays allowed).

### Incumbents
- **Oil, gas and coal companies** will call foreclosure an unconstitutional "taking" of property. Our answer: they keep everything they capture; they only lose what they already let escape.
- **Plugging contractors** lose some government money.
- **Some environmental groups** will oppose anything that powers new data centers with gas.
- **Allies:** data-center developers, satellite companies, tribes and states that lose royalties to waste.

### Unintended consequences, and our guardrails
| Risk | Guardrail |
|---|---|
| **Cobra effect:** creating leaks to win auctions | Only leaks recorded by satellite before a cutoff qualify; owners still pay fines |
| **Strategic abandonment:** dumping wells to buy them back cheap | Former owners stay liable and can't bid on their own sites |
| **Fossil lock-in:** data centers want gas forever | No new drilling, output capped, fixed lease terms |
| **Local smoke:** flares harm neighbors | Fire ranking: use or enclosed destruction near homes; open flares only far from people |
| **Flares go out** (3–5% of the time) | Auto re-igniters, satellite checks, forfeiture if the leak returns |
| **Most methane is outside the U.S.** | Present as a model; buyers can require low-methane gas, as Europe's import rules aim to |
| **Burning makes CO₂** | Already counted: 2.75 t CO₂ per tonne of methane, still 5–30× better than leaking |

## 9. The professor's moves we used
- **Do the opposite:** set leaks on fire instead of plugging every hole.
- **Repurpose an existing tool:** foreclosure, liens and auctions, from real estate and finance.
- **Follow one idea into its neighbors:** from orphaned wells to the whole oil, gas and coal industry.
- **Kill two birds with one stone:** cut methane and power AI.
- **If the math fails, edit the idea:** plugging every well fails cost-benefit, so we target the giants and leave the crumbs.

## 11. Math appendix

| Assumption | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Methane energy | 50 MJ/kg | Standard |
| Gas density | 0.68 kg/m³ | Standard (~15 °C) |
| Generator efficiency | 40% | Assumption |
| Gas price | $3–10 per MMBtu | U.S. vs. Europe/Asia range |
| Warming potential (20-yr / 100-yr) | 82.5 / 29.8 | IPCC AR6, fossil methane |
| Social cost of methane | $2,000/t | EPA (2023), 2025 emissions |
| Average unplugged well | 0.13 t/yr | EPA |
| Real flare efficiency | 91.1% | Plant et al., *Science* 2022 |
| Deaths avoided per Mt cut | ~1,430/yr | UNEP/CCAC Global Methane Assessment |

**Key calculations**
- Leaks: 100 bcm × 0.68 = 68 Mt × 50 MJ/kg = 944 TWh × 40% = **~378 TWh** vs. 415 TWh of data centers (91%)
- One average well: 130 kg/yr × 50 MJ/kg = 1.8 MWh → ~82 W; ~$18/yr of gas; ~$260/yr of climate damage
- One super-emitter: 1 t/hr × 8,766 h = 8,766 t/yr ÷ 0.13 t ≈ **67,000 wells**; ~5.5 MW of power
- Burning: (1 − efficiency) × 82.5 + efficiency × 2.75 t CO₂e per tonne
- Plan totals: sector emissions × share reached × share destroyed (table in section 5); deaths = Mt × 1,430

*All figures are back-of-the-envelope estimates meant to show scale.*
