# Before You Say No, at Least Hear Out the Econ on Data Centers

*Ryker Mirabella · ECON 238, Week One*

There are a lot of headlines about data centers using 70,000 gallons of water every day, or a data center needing a city's worth of electricity. These are true statements, but the context is completely lost in the big numbers, and on their own they are meaningless.

## Big numbers need a comparison

First, large quantities by themselves do not mean anything without comparison. All large organizations require large amounts of resources. A regular golf course in California will use millions of gallons of water a day in the summer. Same thing with electricity: a city of 10,000 people or a city of 100,000 people? Big difference. Also, data centers pay for this electricity like everyone else does. You can be mad that your prices might go up, but paying for what they use keeps the social costs down. So the first thing I take from the numbers is to lower the concern.

## What the company isn't paying for

But not totally. There is concern about what the company is not on the hook for, which someone else will eventually have to pay. There will have to be new power lines for the increased demand for electricity, and normally that cost gets put onto the taxpayer. Basically, you are helping subsidize data center growth. For water, once it is used, nobody tests what it does to the environment or to the neighborhood's uses of the river, which could be negatively affected. Lastly, 30,000 extra cars per day put a real cost on traffic, roadwork, and hassle. Where those cars would be with no data center is another question, but it is still an underlying cost.

## What happens if your town says no

Now suppose your town says no, or waits a year, or heavily taxes it. The demand for computing will just move on to the next-best location and its benefits, possibly a place with looser environmental laws, or a place where you cannot reap the technological benefits. That's not to say every data center or project should be built. It's just that we should be wary of the alternatives if we choose not to cater to demand.

## Three better questions

To the people out there who are worried about their towns: instead of looking at the big numbers, I would focus on three more useful economic questions.

1. **Who is paying for the increase in power?**
2. **What will relieve the added pressure on roadways and on getting to work on time?**
3. **Have we fully cleared the proper water rights to maintain a healthy balance of preservation and productivity?**

Get these questions answered, and the big numbers just do not matter, because they are not a real cost anyway.
