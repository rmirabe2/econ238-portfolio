Opera in the Jungle

The Fall of the Rubber City and the Legend of the Stolen Seeds

In 1896 a city deep in the Amazon opened a European opera house on the profits of wild rubber. Within two decades its fortune was gone, and Brazil blamed an Englishman who carried 70,000 seeds to London. This lecture asks what really beat Brazil: a thief, a fungus, a debt system, or the logic of the boom itself.

About 30 minutes as audio.

The Opera House in the Forest

On the last night of 1896, in a city a thousand miles up the Amazon River, an Italian opera company stepped onto a brand-new stage. The building was the Teatro Amazonas, the Amazon Theatre, in the city of Manaus. A week later, on the seventh of January, 1897, the same company gave the house its first full opera, Ponchielli's La Gioconda.

Think about where that stage stood. On every side, for hundreds of miles, there was rainforest. No road joined Manaus to the rest of Brazil. The bricks, the marble, the glass, and the crystal of the theater had crossed the Atlantic and then traveled up the river by boat. The stage curtain was painted with the meeting of the waters, where the dark Rio Negro joins the muddy Solimões near the city to form the Amazon. Within a few years the city would have electric streetcars. A provincial town at the edge of the forest had decided to become Paris.

What paid for all of it was the milky sap of a tree. Rubber grew wild in the Amazon, and around the turn of the century Brazil sold almost ninety percent of all the rubber traded in the world. The tires of the world's bicycles, and then of its first automobiles, began as drops of sap in the Brazilian forest.

Then comes the story that almost everyone who visits Manaus still hears. In 1876 an Englishman named Henry Wickham gathered seventy thousand rubber seeds on a tributary of the Amazon. He loaded them onto a ship and told the customs officers they were exceedingly delicate botanical specimens, bound for the Queen's own gardens. In London, the seeds were sprouted at the Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew and shipped to Britain's colonies in Asia. There they became plantations, neat rows of trees by the million. In 1913, Asia exported more rubber than Brazil for the first time. The money stopped. The opera company stopped coming. Brazil remembers Wickham as a thief. Britain made him a knight.

It is a perfect story. It has a villain, a heist, and a ruined city. In this lecture, I want to take it apart.

Not because nothing happened in 1876, but because the theft, if it was a theft at all, explains almost nothing about why Brazil lost. The real answer involves a debt that trapped an entire workforce, a forecast printed in the newspapers years ahead of time, a fungus too small to see, and a government that rescued coffee and let rubber go. At heart, this is a story about economics: about what a very high price tells people to do, and who is able to act on it. And it begins with a strange fact. The most valuable tree in the world could not be farmed in its own home.

The Tree That Would Not Be Farmed

The peoples of the Amazon had used rubber for centuries before any European wanted it. For a long time the outside world found it a nuisance. It turned sticky in summer and cracked in winter. That changed in 1839, when the American inventor Charles Goodyear improved a process called vulcanization, which made rubber hold its shape in heat and cold. Suddenly rubber could become hoses, belts, shoe soles, and seals for machines.

What turned a useful material into a gold rush was the bicycle. In 1888 John Dunlop introduced the air-filled bicycle tire, and the bicycle craze of the 1890s put millions of people on rubber. In 1896 the air-filled tire was adapted to the automobile. After 1900, as the car industry grew, demand for rubber climbed faster than anyone had imagined. Harvey Firestone's son later summed up the logic: no rubber meant no tires, and no tires meant no automobiles.

The best rubber came from one species, Hevea brasiliensis, and it grew wild almost only in the Amazon basin. That is the whole secret of Brazil's fortune. It was not a factory or a technique. It was a geographical accident.

But look at how that wild rubber was actually gathered. The trees did not stand together. They were scattered through the forest among thousands of other species. A rubber tapper worked a looping trail through the jungle that might touch more than a hundred trees. He walked it, cutting each trunk and leaving a small cup to catch the sap. Later he walked it again to collect the cups. In the evening he hardened the day's sap into a heavy ball, turning it slowly over a smoky fire. The rains shut the work down for half the year. When the balls were ready, they went downriver by boat to Manaus or to Belém, at the mouth of the Amazon, and from there to London and New York.

Here is the economics. Wild rubber could not grow fast. To produce more, you needed more men walking more trails, deeper into the forest, farther from food and medicine. So when the world's demand jumped, Brazil's supply could only crawl after it, and the gap was filled by price. Rubber became fabulously expensive, and in 1910 it reached its highest price ever.

Economists say supply like this is inelastic: it barely responds to price. When demand rises and supply cannot follow, the price does all the adjusting, and most of the extra money becomes what economists call rent. Rent is income earned not because anyone worked harder or smarter, but because they controlled something scarce. Notice that nobody in Brazil was rigging the market. Thousands of tappers and traders sold rubber, and there was no cartel holding supply back. The scarcity came from the forest. Brazil's rubber fortune was, at its core, a rent the world's bicycle and car makers paid for access to the only place the tree grew wild. And a rent lasts only as long as the scarcity behind it. Nobody in Manaus had invented anything a rival would need to copy. The advantage would vanish the moment someone made the tree grow somewhere else.

Keep that in mind, because it is the hinge of everything that follows. The same fact that made Brazil rich, a valuable tree that grew only wild, also capped how fast Brazil could grow. And a price that high is a signal to the whole world. It says: anyone who can find a cheaper way to make this will become very rich.

The City and the Debt

The man most responsible for the Manaus of the opera house was Eduardo Ribeiro. He was born in 1862 in São Luís, in the northeastern state of Maranhão, and rose as a Black army officer in a country that had abolished slavery only in 1888. As governor of Amazonas in the early 1890s, he drained swamps, laid out broad avenues, and brought the city electric light and running water. He pushed the opera house toward completion, and it opened more than a decade before Rio de Janeiro had a grand opera house of its own. Ribeiro died in Manaus in 1900, in circumstances that have never been fully explained. The city's main avenue still carries his name.

Ribeiro built for a future he could see in the export figures. But the fortune that paid for his city was gathered by people who would never sit in its boxes.

Notice where the money went. Manaus did not spend its fortune on roads into the interior, on farms to feed its workers, or on experiments with planted trees. It spent it on looking European. Ribeiro's dream was a Paris of the tropics, and the city's elite bought that dream piece by piece: marble and crystal from across the ocean, Italian singers, avenues modeled on the boulevards of Europe's capitals. In their world, the trappings of Paris were proof that you were civilized and modern. The men who promoted rubber plantations used the same language of civilization, praising neat rows of trees as progress and calling the tapping economy backward. But for the people who ran Manaus, civilization was something you imported and displayed, not something you planted and waited years for. Culture helped decide what counted as a good use of a fortune.

Most of the tappers were not from the Amazon at all. They came from Brazil's dry Northeast, and especially from the state of Ceará. In the late 1860s the cotton market that sustained the region collapsed, and from 1877 to 1880 a terrible drought destroyed what was left. Tens of thousands fled west, into the forest. By the 1890s, more than twenty thousand Northeasterners a year were heading up the rivers to tap rubber.

They arrived owing money. There were no banks to lend a poor farmer the cost of the journey, so his future employer advanced it. Then the employer advanced him his tools, his food, his clothes, and his ammunition, at prices of the employer's choosing. The tapper paid in rubber, and his rubber was credited at prices of the employer's choosing too. This system was called aviamento, and it ran like a chain. Big export houses in Belém lent to merchants, merchants lent to the owners of rubber estates, and estate owners lent to the tappers. Credit flowed up the river; rubber flowed down it. At the bottom of the chain, a tapper could work for years and owe more than when he arrived, and he was not free to leave until the debt was paid.

Historians argue about how tight the trap really was. Barbara Weinstein, the leading historian of the boom, stressed how estate owners profited by overcharging their workers. The economists Bradford Barham and Oliver Coomes pointed out that small traders traveled the rivers by boat, buying rubber on the side, and that some tappers used them to escape the estate store. The fair reading is that the trap was real, but it leaked.

It helps to see why the system took this shape, because it was not just cruelty. Labor was the scarcest thing in the Amazon. Bringing a worker across Brazil was expensive, and once he was alone in the forest, nobody could watch how hard he worked. Debt solved both problems for the employer. It recovered the cost of the journey, and it tied the worker to one estate. And where the estate store was the only place a tapper could sell his rubber and the only place he could buy his food, the boss stood on both sides of every trade and set both prices. The traders on the river mattered because they broke that power. A second buyer is the beginning of competition.

In 1905 one of Brazil's most famous writers, Euclides da Cunha, led a government expedition up the Purus River, deep in rubber country. In one of the essays he wrote afterward, he described a ritual he saw there. On the Saturday before Easter, the tappers built an effigy of Judas, the traitor, and dressed it as a rubber tapper. Then they set it adrift down the river and shot at it as it floated past. What it meant to the men who did it, we can only guess. But it is hard to hear about it without thinking of men who saw themselves in the figure being punished.

Across the border in Peru, it was worse. In 1910 a British investigator, Roger Casement, found that a London-registered company on the Putumayo River, run by a rubber baron named Julio César Arana, had enslaved and killed thousands of Indigenous people. Arana kept a home in Manaus.

Here is the economic point to carry forward. Every link in the aviamento chain was a loan against tomorrow's wild rubber. The whole region's wealth existed as debts, waiting to be paid in balls of smoked sap. It was a system built to extract. It was not built to invest.

A Crate of Seeds

Now back to the man in the legend. Henry Wickham was born in London in 1846. His father died when he was four. He went to South America young, looking for a fortune, and by the early 1870s he and his wife, Violet, were trying to farm on the Tapajós River, near the town of Santarém. The farm failed. Family members who had followed them to Brazil died. It was not the life of a master criminal. It was the life of a man who kept almost succeeding.

In 1872 Wickham wrote to Sir Joseph Hooker, the director of Kew Gardens, offering to collect plants. Kew and the India Office, the department that governed British India, had wanted rubber seeds for years. Other collectors had sent them, but rubber seeds spoil within days, and the shipments arrived dead. Wickham was offered ten pounds for every thousand seeds.

In the spring of 1876 he gathered them in a hurry from the forest near the village of Boim, on the Tapajós. He got them aboard a steamer bound for Liverpool and declared them to customs as delicate botanical specimens for the Queen's gardens. That much is in the record. But the historian Warren Dean found that Wickham had the permission of the people who ran the rubber groves where he collected. Whether he broke any Brazilian law is far from clear.

The seeds reached Kew in mid-June 1876. Every one was planted. The first sprouts appeared within four days, and by early July about 2,700 had come up. That is fewer than four seeds in a hundred. In August, about 1,900 seedlings were packed into thirty-eight glass plant cases and shipped to Ceylon, today's Sri Lanka. The next year, twenty-two plants reached the botanic garden in Singapore. Wickham was paid a little over seven hundred pounds and sent on his way. Hooker gave him scant credit and would not let him travel with the plants.

And then, for a very long time, almost nothing happened. A rubber tree needs six to eight years before it can be tapped well. And nobody yet knew how to tap a planted tree so that it stayed productive for years. In 1888 a botanist named Henry Ridley became director of the Singapore garden. He developed a way of shaving thin cuts in the bark that kept a tree productive for years, and he spent so long pestering planters to try rubber that they called him Mad Ridley. Commercial planting in the Malay states only began in the mid-1890s, about twenty years after Wickham's crate arrived at Kew.

So the seeds were necessary. Without them, there is no Asian rubber. But they were nowhere near sufficient. Between the crate and the plantations lay two decades of other people's work, and a change in the world market that Wickham had nothing to do with.

There is an economic lesson hidden in those twenty years. Turning a wild tree into a crop took knowledge: how to sprout the seeds, where to grow them, how to tap them without wearing them out. Knowledge like that is what economists call a public good. Once one planter learns it, every other planter can copy it for free, so no single planter has much reason to pay for discovering it. In the British Empire, the state paid. The India Office bought the seeds, Kew sprouted them, and government gardens in Ceylon and Singapore experimented for years before private investors arrived. Brazil's rubber economy invested far less in that kind of learning. The aviamento chain paid people to collect, not to experiment.

Wickham himself drifted from one venture to the next. He planted in Queensland, in British Honduras, and on a small coral atoll off New Guinea, and every one of those ventures failed. Then the rubber plantations began to pay, and Wickham discovered that his one success was worth more as a story. In 1908 he published his own account of 1876. In it, the scheduled steamer became a stroke of providence, and a threatening Brazilian gunboat appeared that is not in the earlier record. In 1911 the planters' associations of Britain, Ceylon, and Malaya held a banquet in his honor, gave him a thousand guineas, and granted him an income for life. In 1920, at the age of seventy-four, he was knighted. The legend of the theft was not something Brazil invented to explain its loss. It was written, in large part, by the man who carried the seeds, at the moment the planters needed a hero. And it suited an empire that celebrated its explorers. A lone Englishman slipping past a gunboat makes a better toast at a banquet than twenty years of botanists fussing over seedlings.

Why Nobody Planted

If the seeds were not the whole story, we have to ask a harder question. Why did no one in Brazil plant rubber in rows while the money was pouring in?

Start with what a plantation actually asks of an investor. You clear the land, plant the trees, bring in workers, and then you wait. For six to eight years the trees produce nothing worth selling. Those costs are sunk. You cannot get them back, and you cannot move the trees to some other use if the price falls.

Now put yourself in Manaus around 1905. You are a rubber merchant, and your fortune is tied up in the aviamento chain, in goods advanced upriver and rubber owed back down. Wild rubber is paying better than ever, this year. Someone proposes a plantation that will earn nothing until about 1912. Economists call the comparison you are making discounting. Money today is worth more than an uncertain promise of money years from now, and the more the present pays, the harder the future has to work to compete. For a Manaus merchant, the present was paying spectacularly. Saying no to the plantation was not stupid. It was the reasonable answer to the question he was being asked.

Economists see something else at work in booms like this, and they give it an odd name: Dutch disease, after the troubles that followed natural gas discoveries in the Netherlands in the 1960s. When one export pays extraordinarily well, it pulls workers, money, and attention away from everything else. Why grow food in the Amazon when a man can tap rubber? So the region imported its food, and the tappers' supplies were shipped in from far away at great expense, which made Brazilian rubber costly to produce. The economists Bradford Barham and Oliver Coomes have read the rubber boom through exactly this lens. The boom did not just fail to build a lasting economy. It crowded one out.

The Asian planters faced the same arithmetic, and for a long time it stopped them too. Through the 1880s and 1890s, wild rubber kept up with demand, and nobody could foresee how big the car industry would become. What changed their minds was the automobile. Around 1905, with prices at historic highs, European investors began planting in earnest. Across Asia, the area under rubber grew from about fifteen thousand acres in 1901 to more than four hundred thousand in 1907. Those trees would mature around 1913.

And Asia had more than trees. In Brazil, by one estimate around 1910, it took 337 pounds of investment to put a tapper to work. On an Asian plantation it took about 210. The plantation tapper may have been up to eighty percent more productive, in part because his trees stood a few steps apart instead of scattered through miles of forest. There was no rainy season to stop the tapping. Planters provided basic medical care, while tappers in the Amazon faced yellow fever and malaria alone. And the planters drew on a vast, cheap labor supply, recruited above all from the drought-prone districts of South India. Many of those Tamil workers were bound by debt to the estates. Look closely and you find the same story on both sides of the world. In Brazil, drought refugees from the Northeast were trapped by debt. In Malaya, drought refugees from South India were trapped by debt. The labor systems were more alike than different.

Now for the awkward fact. By 1910, the danger was no longer invisible. That year, rubber shares set off a frenzy on the London Stock Exchange. On a single day in March 1910, ten new rubber companies issued shares in London. Between 1909 and 1914, a hundred and forty-five rubber companies launched there and raised nearly thirteen million pounds. Newspapers as far away as New Zealand compared it to the South Sea Bubble, the famous financial crash of 1720. And in March 1910, a report on the boom said plainly that if these new companies carried out their planting plans, the world's supply of rubber would see an immense increase five or six years later. They were almost exactly right.

That same year, rubber interests met in Manaus for a congress on the industry. Prices were so high that nobody felt any urgency. The future had been printed in the newspapers, and the present was too profitable to hear it.

And even a merchant who believed the forecast faced a trap. If he stopped advancing goods upriver, his rivals would gladly take his tappers and his rubber. Acting alone, no merchant could change what was coming. Acting together would have required an institution the north did not have, one that could make everyone invest for the future at the same time.

The Fungus

There is still one thing the incentive story cannot explain. When the danger became obvious, why didn't Brazil simply plant its own trees in rows and fight back? Brazilians tried. Again and again, in the Amazon, the plantations failed. For decades the usual explanation was social. Brazilian capitalists were said to be too timid, and Brazilian workers unwilling to accept wage labor on a plantation.

Warren Dean, the historian we met with Wickham's seeds, made a different argument. The problem, he said, was mainly ecological. In the Amazon, the rubber tree lives alongside a parasite, a fungus now called South American leaf blight. It attacks only rubber trees. In the wild, that hardly matters, because the trees are scattered and the fungus struggles to travel from one to the next. Plant the trees close together, and you have built the fungus a feast. It strips the young leaves, the trees weaken, and the plantation dies.

That changes how we see everything. The blight exists throughout tropical Central and South America. It has never reached Asia or Africa. The planters of Malaya were not simply better managers. They were farming a tree that had left its enemy behind on the other side of the world.

Dean's argument is convenient for Brazil's rubber elite, because it blames nature instead of them. So it deserves a test. And history provided one, though it came after the boom had ended. In 1928 Henry Ford, the most famous industrialist in America, after British planters had spent years restricting supply to prop up prices, began building his own plantation town in the Amazon. He called it Fordlândia, and he put it on the Tapajós, the same river where Wickham had gathered his seeds. Here was everything Brazil supposedly lacked: unlimited capital, modern management, and wage labor.

Fordlândia failed, and not only because of nature. Ford never consulted an expert on growing rubber. His managers planted on hilly, thin soil that the rains washed away. But the blight and a crowd of insects tore through the closely planted trees. Ford tried again at a better site downriver, called Belterra. There, between 1934 and 1942, his company planted about sixty-five hundred hectares, using trees brought from Asia and bred to resist disease. The blight returned anyway. In 1945, Ford's grandson sold both towns to the Brazilian government at a loss of more than twenty million dollars. Goodyear tried in Panama in 1935 and gave up five years later.

Meanwhile Firestone, Ford's rival, planted rubber in Liberia, in West Africa, at about the same time. It succeeded almost immediately. Same tree, same era, one difference. There was no leaf blight in Africa.

Economists would call this pair a natural experiment. Ford and Firestone had the same tree, the same years, the same deep pockets, and the same hunger for tires. The main thing that differed was the fungus. That does not prove the blight was the only obstacle, because Ford's own mistakes were real. But it does show that money and management were not enough in the Amazon, and that they were enough where the fungus was absent.

Even today, the blight is the biggest obstacle to rubber plantations in Brazil.

So now we have two halves of an answer, and they fit together. During the boom, the incentives made planting look foolish, so Brazil's merchants did not seriously try. When it was too late, and they did try, the forest itself defeated them. Neither half needs a thief.

The Record Year

The end, when it came, came with a cruel sense of timing. In 1912, Brazil exported more wild rubber than in any year in its history, more than forty-two thousand tons. The next year, Asia's exports passed Brazil's for the first time, about fifty-four thousand tons to Brazil's thirty-six thousand. By 1914, Asia was shipping more than twice as much as Brazil. Prices, which had peaked in 1910, kept falling until they were far below where they stood before the boom began.

Brazil did not simply watch. In August 1911 the government debated a rescue, and on the fifth of January, 1912, Brazil's Congress approved a Rubber Defense Plan. On paper, it was exactly the long-term thinking the boom had lacked. It offered prizes and incentives to anyone who would plant rubber trees and process rubber properly. It promised health care for migrant workers, a railroad in the rubber territory of Acre, and lower export taxes. It was one of the first serious regional development plans in Brazil's history.

It was never carried out. One of the few pieces that happened was a team of doctors from the Oswaldo Cruz Institute, who traveled the rubber rivers from late 1912 into 1913 studying the region's diseases. By one historian's account, the plan lasted seventeen months.

Why? Part of the answer is that it arrived too late. A tree planted in 1912 could not be tapped until about 1920, and by then the market was gone. But Brazil had rescued another crop from a crisis, and that comparison tells us more.

In 1906, coffee prices had collapsed from overproduction. Coffee was grown in the south, around São Paulo, and the coffee states agreed to buy up coffee and store it, keeping it off the market until the price recovered. The national government came to back the scheme. Coffee was saved, and rubber was not.

The first reason is power. The coffee states, above all São Paulo and Minas Gerais, dominated Brazil's national politics in these years. The rubber north was remote, thinly populated, and weak in the capital. The historians who studied the rubber plan describe it as caught between ambitions for development and plain political neglect.

The second reason is economics, and it is less comfortable. Stockpiling works when you control most of a market. Brazil grew most of the world's coffee, so holding coffee back really could push the price up. Rubber's problem was different. A cheaper competitor had arrived. If Brazil held its rubber off the market, the buyers would simply go to Asia, and stay there. To compete, Brazil would have had to make wild rubber cheaper. That meant lower costs, which meant feeding and supplying tappers far more cheaply, which meant building roads, farms, and towns across the Amazon. Economic historians have concluded that such an expensive solution made little sense in the 1910s, when coffee and the new factories of São Paulo offered far better returns.

So the national government's neglect had two faces. It was a political choice that favored the powerful south. And it was also, from the point of view of the whole country, a judgment about opportunity cost. Every mil-réis spent rebuilding the Amazon was one not spent in São Paulo, where it would earn more. That judgment was hard to argue with. The tragedy of the north was that both could be true at the same time.

Back to the Opera House

Go back now to that stage in Manaus, on the last night of 1896. The Italian singers, the marble from across the ocean, the electric lights in the middle of the forest. When the money stopped, the house went quiet. For most of the twentieth century it saw little grand opera. In 1982 the German director Werner Herzog filmed Fitzcarraldo, the story of a rubber-boom dreamer obsessed with bringing opera to the jungle, and by then the Manaus theater stood as a symbol of a vanished world. Only in the late 1990s did a yearly opera festival fill the house again.

Henry Wickham died in London in 1928, Sir Henry by then. By the year of his death, there were eighty million rubber trees growing in British territories where none had grown before.

So was Manaus robbed? Here is the verdict the evidence supports. Something was taken in 1876, a crate of seeds, perhaps with permission, carried off at the request of an empire that wanted them. Without those seeds, Asia's plantations would have come later, or from some other route. But the seeds did not ruin Brazil. They sat in botanic gardens for twenty years. What ruined Brazil's rubber economy was a set of forces no thief could have arranged. The boom was so profitable that planting looked foolish. The economy was organized around debt and wild trees, and it could not grow faster or cheaper. A fungus made plantations nearly impossible in the Amazon itself. And when the end came, the national government saved coffee and let rubber go, for reasons of power and reasons of sense alike.

The theft story lasted because everyone who told it gained something. For Brazil's rubber elite, it turned a defeat into a crime and spared them the question of what they had done with their fortunes. For the British planters, it gave a new industry a heroic founder at the very moment they were selling shares. For Wickham, it turned a lifetime of failures into one great success.

And the people who actually decided how the story ended never got a banquet. The drought refugees from Ceará walking their trails before the rains came. The Tamil workers on the Malayan estates. The botanists who learned to tap a planted tree. And a fungus too small to see, which did more to settle the fate of the Amazon than any Englishman with a crate.

There is a lesson here, and it is not a simple one about greed. It is about how a windfall feels from the inside. When a fortune comes from a lucky accident of nature, it feels, to the people living on it, like a talent. Economists would say Manaus was living on a rent and mistaking it for productivity. The warning was printed in the newspapers in 1910. The rescue plan was passed at the peak. The people of Manaus were not foolish. They were answering the question the market asked them each year, and every year the answer was the same: take the money now. Economies built on a single resource have faced that same question many times since, and it has never been an easy one to answer well.

And the story is not finished. Asia and Africa now grow more than ninety-five percent of the world's natural rubber, and they are still free of the leaf blight. Plant scientists rank it among the most threatening plant diseases in the world, and they warn that the danger rises as more people and goods travel between the American tropics and Asia. Since 1956, Asian governments have cooperated on measures to keep the fungus out. Notice what kind of problem that is. It is a public good, like the knowledge sprouted at Kew: every rubber grower benefits from keeping the blight away, and no single grower can pay to do it alone. The accident of nature that handed Asia the boom has to be guarded today like a border. The advantage that ended the Rubber City's fortune could, one day, end someone else's.

The opera house still stands. It has been restored, it is on UNESCO's World Heritage list, and opera is sung there again. It was never a monument to a theft. It is a monument to a city that mistook a gift of the forest for a fortune it had made, and that built, while the gift lasted, something beautiful enough to outlive it.
