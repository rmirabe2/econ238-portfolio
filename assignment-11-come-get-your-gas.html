Come Get Your Gas, or I'll Light It on Fire 🔥

Leak it, lose it: foreclosing on methane leaks to recover ~54 billion cubic meters of gas a year, enough to power half of today's data centers.

View the infographic

The idea in one sentence: if you let your gas leak, you lose it. Someone else can capture it, and if nobody wants it, we set it on fire, because a burning leak is 8–30 times better for the climate than a silent one.

1. The hook: the Gates of Hell

In 1971, Soviet scientists hit a pocket of gas in Turkmenistan's Karakum Desert and set it on fire, expecting it to burn out in weeks. It has been burning for more than 50 years. People call it the Gates of Hell.

It looks like an environmental disaster. It's actually the opposite. Burning methane turns it into CO₂, and methane traps about 80 times more heat than CO₂ over 20 years. A burning leak is 8 to 30 times better for the climate than a silent one (it's never perfect: some methane always escapes). Meanwhile, Turkmenistan is one of the world's worst methane leakers (IEA, 2026). Its real damage comes from the leaks nobody lit.

Now Turkmenistan has drilled wells nearby to divert the crater's gas, its scientists say some of it could be captured for industrial use, and the fire's heat has dropped more than 75% in three years (Capterio). The crater tells the whole story: leaking is worst, burning is better, using it is best. So why does the world still let so much gas leak?

2. The problem: 124 million tonnes, and nobody owns it

The oil, gas and coal industries released 124 million tonnes of methane in 2025 (IEA Global Methane Tracker 2026):

Source Methane Share
Oil 45 Mt 36%
Coal 43 Mt 35%
Natural gas 36 Mt 29%

Why it leaks: nobody owns a leak. - Inside the pipe, the gas belongs to the company. Once it escapes, it belongs to no one, so leaking costs the company nothing while everyone else gets the warming and the smog. - At remote wells with no pipeline, the gas is worth $0 to the operator, so wasting it feels free.

Why the usual fixes fail: - Fines become a price. In Nigeria's Niger Delta, companies cut flaring only about 10% since 2007, preferring to pay fines rather than stop. - Selling the problem away. When Shell sold Nigerian fields, flaring at Oyigbo rose about 15× while Shell reported zero routine flaring. Responsibility followed the owner, not the leak. - The real owners lose. On the Fort Berthold Reservation in North Dakota, the tribe owns royalties on gas that companies burn instead of capturing.

This is a property-rights failure. Leaked gas has no owner, so no one has a reason to save it.

3. Not every leak is worth burning

A few giant leaks do much of the damage. Across about 15,000 measurements from 18 studies, the largest 5% of leaks typically caused over 50% of all leakage (Brandt et al. 2016). In the Permian Basin, super-emitters cause ~50% (37–73%) of emissions (Cusworth et al. 2026). Leak size decides what to do:

Leak size Share of sites Share of methane Why What to do
Small ~75–85% ~10–25% Fails cost-benefit. An average orphaned well leaks ~$18 of gas and does ~$260 of climate damage a year; a fix costs ~$20,000, and burners can't run on leaks this tiny. Plugging cost more than its benefit in 75–80% of measured wells (RFF 2026). Leave it (unless near homes or water)
Medium ~15–25% ~30–45% The sweet spot. Too small to sell, big enough to burn; a ~$19,000 oxidizer pays back its climate damage in under 2 years on a ~6 t/yr leak. Burn it
Super-emitters (over ~100 kg/hour) ~1–5% ~40–60% Too valuable to burn. One 1-tonne-per-hour leak can power a small data center (~5.5 MW) and is worth $1.2–4.2M of gas a year. Capture it and power a data center

Exception: coal mine ventilation air is too thin to use (under 1% methane), so those giants get burned in large oxidizers. Share-of-sites and medium/small ranges are our estimates from U.S. studies; super-emitter ranges are measured.

Why bring the data center to the leak? As Crusoe, the company that pioneered this, put it: "moving data is much easier and cheaper than moving gas." A remote leak has no pipeline, but it only needs an internet connection to sell computing.

Companies could already fix many of these for free, and choose not to. The IEA finds that around 30% of fossil-fuel methane could be cut at no cost, and that nearly 30 Mt of upstream oil and gas emissions could be eliminated at no net cost at 2025 gas prices. The gas saved pays for the repair, and the leaks continue anyway.

Ignore the crumbs. Burn the middle. Plug the giants into data centers.

4. The plan: leaked gas is abandoned property

The rule: any methane that escapes is abandoned property. If you don't treat a leak, you can lose the site's gas to someone who will, like a foreclosure on a house you stopped paying for.

How it works

  1. Detect. Satellites (MethaneSAT, Carbon Mapper, the UN's MARS system) and inspections flag leaks and post them on a public list.
  2. Notice. The owner gets 60 days to fix it and keep its gas.
  3. Foreclose. If not:
  4. Active sites (oil fields, pipelines, coal mines): the right to capture the leaking gas is auctioned. The winner installs the equipment at the owner's cost and keeps the gas. The owner still pays any fines.
  5. Abandoned sites (orphaned wells, closed mines): no owner to warn, so they go straight to auction, with no inherited liability for the winner.
  6. No bidder? Medium and larger leaks get burned, paid for by the owner or by auction revenue. Tiny leaks are left alone.

Likely outcomes by sector

Sector Share of methane Most likely outcome
Oil and gas near pipelines most of 65% The owner fixes it. The gas is worth more than the repair, so the threat alone does the job.
Oil and gas, remote (no pipeline) part of 65% A data center wins the auction and runs on the stranded gas.
Coal mine ventilation air most of 35% No bidder (too thin to sell), so it's burned in large oxidizers at the owner's cost.
Coal mine drained gas part of 35% Auctioned: sold, or used to power a data center.
Abandoned coal mines ~3% Auctioned if the gas is rich enough, otherwise burned.
Orphaned wells ~0.3% Medium ones burned, tiny ones left alone.

The best foreclosure is the one that never happens. Most of the impact comes from owners fixing leaks before they lose them.

Where fire is allowed

  1. Use it (sell it, or burn it inside a generator): required near homes.
  2. Destroy it cleanly (enclosed combustors, flameless oxidizers).
  3. Open flare: last resort, only far from people.
  4. Vent it: never.

Rules for auction winners

  • No new holes: no drilling or pumping to boost the flow; output capped at the leak rate.
  • Use it on site: the gas powers the data center or plant; it can't become a new gas field.
  • Pay for results: if satellites show the leak back, the winner loses the claim and its bond.
  • Clean exit: part of the revenue goes into a fund to seal the site when the gas runs out.

The money loop

Auctions of valuable sites raise money. That money pays to burn the worthless leaks (coal exhaust, orphaned wells) and goes partly to the communities living next to the leaks.

  • The ceiling: the IEA finds about 70% of fossil methane (nearly 85 Mt) can be cut with existing technology, including three-quarters of oil and gas emissions and about half of coal.
  • Recovered gas counts only oil and gas leaks that are captured and used; burned coal gas is destroyed, not recovered. Data-center comparison: all data centers used 415 TWh in 2024 (IEA).
  • Side benefit: methane forms ground-level ozone; each million tonnes cut prevents about 1,430 premature deaths a year (UNEP/CCAC Global Methane Assessment).
  • For comparison: the Global Methane Pledge aims for a 30% cut by 2030. Even our low case matches it.
  • Why not 100%: satellites only see big leaks, some coal methane can't be captured with today's technology, and burning isn't perfect (real flares destroy about 91%).

About 45% of fossil methane, gone, and ~54 billion cubic meters of gas recovered every year: enough to power half of today's data centers.

6. Proof it can work

Every piece of the plan has already been done somewhere. Nobody has put the pieces together.

Piece of the plan Where it's already happening
Foreclose and auction leaked gas Nigeria
Power data centers with waste gas Crusoe, United States
Burn gas too thin to sell Coal mine oxidizers
Burn medium leaks without a flame Catalytic oxidizers, Canada
Capture abandoned mine gas Germany
Capture leaks at scale North Dakota
  • Nigeria: its Gas Flare Commercialisation Programme takes gas oil companies were flaring and auctions it. 49 flare sites were auctioned to 42 bidders, to capture 250–300 million cubic feet a day and cut ~6 million tonnes of CO₂ a year. Lesson: it works, but slowly. The program sat dormant for seven years before awards.
  • Crusoe: "moving data is much easier and cheaper than moving gas." At one point it ran about 125 modular data centers on waste gas, cutting methane ~98% versus flaring. Lesson: it pays, but the computer must match the leak. In 2025 Crusoe sold the business (425 units) to NYDIG to chase giant AI campuses.
  • Coal mine oxidizers: regenerative thermal oxidizers are the most proven way to destroy thin mine-ventilation methane, and fully abating coal mine methane worldwide would cost under $4 billion (Ember 2026).
  • Catalytic oxidizers: in Canadian tests, flameless units destroyed 78–94% of the methane from a leaking well vent.
  • Germany: captures and uses about 99% of the methane from its abandoned coal mines, mostly for electricity.
  • North Dakota: once pipelines and rules caught up, companies captured 95.5% of Bakken gas in a recent month.

Nigeria auctions it. Crusoe computes on it. Germany powers homes with it. Nobody has put it together.

7. Economic analysis

Incentives

Player Today Under "Leak it, lose it"
Leak owners Leaking is free Leak → lose the gas, pay for the fix, plus fines. Cheapest move: fix it yourself.
Absent or bankrupt owners No one responsible Auction winners profit from capping the leak
Data centers and gas buyers Wait years for power Cheap power, available now
Governments Pay $20,000–76,000 per old well Collect auction revenue; spend it on the leaks that matter
Communities near leaks Breathe the smoke, get nothing Share of auction revenue; open flares banned near homes

The key shift: today, leaking is free and fixing costs money. Under the plan, leaking costs more than fixing.

Why hasn't anyone done this? (Chesterton's fence)

  1. Escaped gas has no owner, so no one has standing to claim it.
  2. "Touch it, you own it." Cleaning up an old site can mean inheriting its liability. The U.S. Good Samaritan Act (2024) shields volunteers, but allows only 15 cleanups in a 7-year pilot and bans profit.
  3. You couldn't see the leaks. Satellites that spot individual leaks from space are only a few years old.
  4. No pipelines at remote wells, so the gas looked worthless.

The fence has real purposes, and we keep them: plugging protects groundwater (so we still plug near homes and water), and flares exist for safety (so emergency flaring stays allowed).

Who pays today (environmental justice)

  • Everyone, a little: methane forms smog that kills people far from any leak. The plan avoids ~80,000 of those deaths a year (middle case).
  • Front-line communities, a lot:
  • Niger Delta, Nigeria: ~2 million people live within 4 km of a gas flare; World Bank researchers link flaring to illness among young children. Companies paid fines rather than stop.
  • Fort Berthold Reservation, North Dakota: the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara Nation saw ~20% of the state's flaring from 2012 to 2020. The tribe owns royalties on that gas, loses them when it's burned, and breathes the smoke.
  • How the plan helps: part of the auction money goes to host communities, open flares are banned near homes, and the right to the gas is tied to the site, so selling a field can't make the problem disappear on paper.

External benefits of the plan: fewer deaths, higher crop yields from less smog, and new revenue for the communities living next to leaks.

Incumbents and the fights ahead

  • Oil, gas and coal companies will call foreclosure an unconstitutional "taking." Our answer: they keep all gas they capture; they lose only what they already let escape.
  • Plugging contractors lose some government spending.
  • Some environmental groups will oppose powering data centers with fossil gas. Our answer: this gas is already escaping. Using it beats leaking it, and no new drilling is allowed.
  • Likely allies: data-center developers, satellite companies, and tribes and states losing royalties.

Unintended consequences, and the guardrails

Risk Guardrail
Cobra effect: creating leaks to win auctions Only leaks seen by satellite before a cutoff date qualify; owners still pay fines
Strategic abandonment: dumping wells to buy them back cheap Former owners stay liable and can't bid on their own sites
Fossil lock-in: data centers want gas forever No new drilling, output capped at the leak rate, fixed lease terms
Smoke next door Fire ranking: use the gas or burn it in enclosed units near homes; open flares only far from people
Flares go out (3–5% of the time) Auto re-igniters, satellite checks, lose the claim if the leak returns
Most methane is outside the U.S. A model for others; buyers can demand low-methane gas, as Europe is starting to
Burning makes CO₂ Already counted: still 8–30× better than leaking

Biggest drawback: enforcement

Nigeria's program took seven years to start, and some countries ignore their own flaring laws. Best economic answer: the plan doesn't rely on regulators winning every fight. It creates bidders who profit from enforcing it, because every ignored leak is money for someone else. Self-interest does the policing.

8. The professor's moves we used

  • Do the opposite: set leaks on fire instead of plugging every hole.
  • Repurpose an existing tool: foreclosure, liens and auctions, borrowed from real estate and finance.
  • Kill two birds with one stone: cut methane and power AI with the same gas.
  • Follow one idea into its neighbors: from one burning crater, to orphaned wells, to the whole oil, gas and coal industry.
  • If the math fails, edit the idea: plugging every well fails cost-benefit, so we leave the crumbs and target the giants.
